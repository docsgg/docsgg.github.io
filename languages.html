Languages & Scripts

How to identify regions by the writing system. Recognizing the script family is often enough to make a confident guess.

Europe

Latin Alphabet (Most of Europe)

Most European languages use the Latin alphabet, but unique letters and diacritics give away specific countries.

  • German: ä, ö, ü, ß. Used in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Liechtenstein, Belgium, and Namibia (Africa).
  • French: ç, é, è, ê, à, ù, â, î, ô, û, ë, ï, ü, ÿ, œ, æ. Used in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Canada (Quebec), and many African countries (Senegal, Tunisia, etc.).
  • Spanish: ñ, ¿, ¡. Used in Spain, Mexico, and most of Central/South America (except Brazil).
  • Portuguese: ã, õ, ç, á, é, í, ó, ú, â, ê, ô. Used in Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe, East Timor, and Macau.
  • Italian: Uses standard Latin, but common words like via, piazza, uscita, chiuso are giveaways. Used in Italy, San Marino, Vatican City, and parts of Switzerland.
  • Polish: ą, ć, ę, ł, ń, ó, ś, ź, ż. Used in Poland.
  • Czech/Slovak: č, ď, ě, ň, ř, š, ť, ž, ů. Used in Czechia and Slovakia.
  • Hungarian: ő, ű (unique to Hungarian). Used in Hungary and parts of Romania/Slovakia.
  • Romanian: ă, â, î, ș, ț. Used in Romania and Moldova.
  • Turkish: ç, ğ, ı, İ, ö, ş, ü. Used in Turkey and Cyprus.
  • Nordic: Å, Æ, Ø (Danish/Norwegian), Ä, Ö (Swedish/Finnish). Used in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Iceland.
  • Baltic: Latvian uses ā, ē, ī, ū, ģ, ķ, ļ, ņ; Lithuanian uses ą, č, ę, ė, į, š, ų, ū, ž.
Spanish vs Portuguese: Spanish uses ñ and ¿/¡. Portuguese uses ã, õ, and ç. If you see "PARE" on a stop sign, it's likely Spanish-speaking South America (Brazil also uses PARE sometimes, but Portuguese has ç/ã).

Cyrillic Alphabet

Used in Eastern Europe, the Balkans, and Central Asia. Unique letters are the key to telling countries apart.

  • Russian: ы, э, ё. Used in Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Belarus (alongside Belarusian).
  • Ukrainian: і, ї, є, ґ. Used in Ukraine.
  • Belarusian: ў (unique). Used in Belarus.
  • Bulgarian: ъ used as a normal vowel (e.g., България). Used in Bulgaria.
  • Serbian: ћ, ђ, ј, љ, њ, џ. Used in Serbia, Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina.
  • Macedonian: ѓ, ќ, ѕ, ј, љ, њ, џ. Used in North Macedonia.
  • Kazakh: ә, ғ, қ, ң, ө, ұ, ү, һ, і. Used in Kazakhstan.
  • Kyrgyz: ң, ө, ү (without ә or ұ). Used in Kyrgyzstan.
  • Mongolian: ө, ү (without ң). Used in Mongolia.
Russian vs Ukrainian: Russian uses ы, э, ё. Ukrainian uses і, ї, є, ґ. If you see і (with a dot), it's not Russian.

Greek Alphabet

Unique and easy to recognize. Letters like α, β, γ, δ, ε, λ, π, σ, ω are unmistakable.

  • Greek: Used in Greece and Cyprus.

Asia

East Asian Scripts

  • Japanese: A mix of Kanji (Chinese characters), Hiragana (curvy), and Katakana (angular). Used in Japan.
  • Korean (Hangul): Built from square-ish blocks with circles (ㅇ, ㅎ). Used in South Korea.
  • Chinese: Simplified characters used in Mainland China and Singapore. Traditional characters used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau. Mainland China has no Street View coverage.
  • Vietnamese: Latin alphabet with many tone marks and diacritics (đ, ă, â, ê, ô, ơ, ư). Used in Vietnam.
Japanese vs Chinese: If you see Hiragana (curvy) or Katakana (angular), it's Japan. If you only see dense, complex characters, it could be Chinese (Taiwan/Hong Kong) or Japanese Kanji. Korea is easy: look for circles in the blocks.

Southeast Asian Scripts

  • Thai: Curvy script with small loops and descending lines. Used in Thailand.
  • Khmer: Intricate, dense, and ornate. Used in Cambodia.
  • Lao: Similar to Thai but simpler. Used in Laos.
  • Burmese: Circular, rounded letters. Used in Myanmar (limited coverage).
  • Indonesian/Malay: Latin alphabet. Indonesian uses "ng", "ny", "ai", "au". Malay sometimes has Jawi (Arabic) script but mostly Latin. Used in Indonesia and Malaysia.
Thai vs Lao vs Khmer: Thai has small loops and a line at the top. Khmer is very dense and ornate (looks like little "among us" shapes). Lao is simpler than Thai.

South Asian Scripts

  • Hindi: Devanagari script, with a continuous line across the top (like a clothesline). Used in India.
  • Bengali: Similar continuous headline, but different character shapes. Used in Bangladesh and India (West Bengal).
  • Sinhala: Very rounded, circular shapes. Used in Sri Lanka.
  • Tamil: Angular, rounded forms. Used in Sri Lanka, India, and Singapore.
  • Nepali: Devanagari script (like Hindi). Used in Nepal.
  • Dzongkha: Tibetan script. Used in Bhutan.
Hindi vs Bengali: Both have a top line. Bengali characters look like squiggles with little circles. Hindi characters are more angular and "hanging".

Middle East & Central Asia

  • Arabic: Right-to-left, joined script with dots above/below. Used in UAE, Qatar, Oman, Jordan, Lebanon, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, etc.
  • Hebrew: Square-ish letters, right-to-left. Used in Israel.
  • Persian (Farsi): Arabic script with extra letters (پ, چ, ژ, گ). Used in Iran (no coverage).
  • Urdu: Arabic script with extra letters (ٹ, ڈ, ڑ, ں, ے, ہ, ھ). Used in Pakistan.
Arabic vs Persian/Urdu: If you see پ, چ, ژ, گ, it's not Arabic (it's Persian, Urdu, or Pashto). Urdu has additional letters like ٹ and ں.

Africa

Major Languages

  • French: Used in Senegal, Tunisia, Madagascar, and many West/Central African countries.
  • English: Used in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, South Africa, Uganda, Tanzania, Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho, Malawi, Rwanda, Namibia.
  • Portuguese: Used in Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe.
  • Arabic: Used in Egypt, Tunisia, and North Africa.
  • Afrikaans: Latin alphabet with unique words. Used in South Africa and Namibia.

Americas

North America & Caribbean

  • English: USA, Canada (except Quebec), and Caribbean nations.
  • French: Canada (Quebec), Haiti (alongside Creole).
  • Spanish: Mexico, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Costa Rica, Panama, Guatemala.
  • Dutch: Curaçao (alongside Papiamento), Aruba, Sint Maarten.

South America

  • Spanish: Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Uruguay, Paraguay, Venezuela.
  • Portuguese: Brazil.
  • Guaraní: Co-official in Paraguay.
Spanish vs Portuguese: Look for "PARE" (Spanish) vs "PARE" (Portuguese, but with ç/ã elsewhere). Portuguese uses ã, õ, ç; Spanish uses ñ, ¿, ¡.

Oceania

Languages

  • English: Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu.
  • Maori: Co-official in New Zealand (Latin script with macrons: ā, ē, ī, ō, ū).
